The study, [*Colocalization of eQTLs With Type 2 Diabetes and Glycemic Traits Using Whole-Genome Sequences in Diverse Populations From the NHLBI Trans-Omics in Precision Medicine (TOPMed) Program*](https://doi.org/10.2337/db25-0557), examined the genetic architecture of Type 2 diabetes (T2D) and related glycemic traits using high-coverage whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from the NHLBI Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) Program.

Led by Ningyuan Wang, a graduate student at the Boston University School of Public Health, and Dr. Alisa Manning, assistant professor at Harvard Medical School and associate member of the Broad Institute, the research team analyzed data from tens of thousands of participants representing diverse ancestral populations. By including individuals from multiple ancestries, the study addressed longstanding limitations of genetic research that has relied primarily on populations of European descent.

Researchers used single-variant association testing, fine mapping and colocalization analyses to examine how genetic variation influences T2D and related blood sugar traits. The analysis identified 76 distinct genetic association signals across 34 loci, including novel variants associated with Type 2 diabetes at the **DUSP9** gene locus and with HbA1c at the **ROBO1**, **NDN** and **MYT1** gene loci.

The team also integrated whole-genome sequencing data with gene expression information from diabetes-relevant tissues, identifying 80 regulatory colocalization signals that provide new insight into how genetic variation influences diabetes biology.

<Blockquote>
By integrating large-scale whole-genome sequencing with expression data across ancestrally Blockquoteerse populations, we've been able to pinpoint 80 regulatory colocalization signals in diabetes-relevant tissues," said lead author Ningyuan Wang. "This provides concrete functional links between these genetic variations and diabetes physiology, bringing us closer to precise, actionable therapeutic targets.
</Blockquote>

## Enabling Large-Scale Genomic Analysis With BDC

The scale and complexity of the study required secure access to large genomic datasets and substantial computing resources. Rather than downloading and managing massive whole-genome sequencing files locally, researchers used BDC-Seven Bridges and BDC-Terra to access TOPMed data directly in the cloud and perform the study's primary single-variant association analyses.

By bringing researchers to the data, BDC provided the scalable cloud computing environment needed to process thousands of whole-genome sequences while supporting collaboration across multiple institutions.

<Blockquote attribution="Dr. Alisa Manning, Senior author ">
  BDC's platforms allowed our team to efficiently access the TOPMed data and run our primary association workflows in the cloud ... It provided the scalable computing infrastructure needed to handle a project of this magnitude and enabled seamless collaboration across institutions, allowing us to uncover the regulatory mechanisms driving diabetes risk.
</Blockquote>

Recognition as the August 2026 *Diabetes* Paper of the Month underscores the value of combining diverse genomic datasets with cloud-based analysis platforms to advance biomedical discovery. As studies continue to grow in size and complexity, BDC helps researchers securely analyze large-scale datasets, collaborate across institutions and generate insights that may inform future approaches to disease prevention, diagnosis and treatment.

### Researcher Profiles

**Ningyuan Wang is a Ph.D.** candidate in Biostatistics at Boston University whose research focuses on developing statistical methodologies to advance precision medicine. Working at the intersection of high-dimensional statistics and statistical genetics and genomics, she develops robust analytical frameworks for the integration and interpretation of complex biological and clinical data, with particular emphasis on mediation analysis of multi-omics data to investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying complex diseases such as Type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer's disease. In addition to research, Ningyuan is committed to graduate education and mentorship, having taught advanced biostatistics courses and mentored students across mathematics, computer science, and biostatistics. Her teaching emphasizes statistical rigor, methodological integrity, reproducible research, and the responsible use of artificial intelligence in scientific inquiry.

**Alisa Manning, PhD** is an Instructor of Medicine at Harvard Medical School, an Associated Scientist at Program in Medical and Population Genetics at Broad Institute, and an Assistant Investigator at Clinical Translational Epidemiology Unit at Mass General.

Dr. Manning is a statistical geneticist with an established focus on glycemic traits and a recent focus on integrative analyses of whole genome sequence data. She has been exploring the link between genetic associations and phenotype refinement since 2005\. Her lab studies the molecular and mechanistic basis of insulin resistance and obesity and is known for developing statistical strategies for translational epidemiology using whole genome sequence association studies, physiological and molecular studies in humans and integrative analytics, with the overarching goal to understand the relationship between Genes, Environment and Disease and to foster a stimulating and interdisciplinary research environment with local and international collaborators on a range of projects.
